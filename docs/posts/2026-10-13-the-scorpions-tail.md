# The Scorpion’s Tail: Vemana's Warning on the Envious Courtier

![The Vedic Rishi Instructing Disciple on the Nature of Venom and the Full-Bodied Malice of Toxic Courtiers](../assets/images/posts/2026-10-13-the-scorpions-tail.jpg)

The early morning sunlight filtered through the ancient canopy of the hermitage, casting soft, dappled shadows across the stone courtyard. Madhava, a seasoned administrator from the imperial revenue registry, ascended the path with heavy, hesitant steps. His shoulders were slumped, his face lined with the deep fatigue that stems not from long hours of labor, but from steady, unexplained sabotage by someone close at hand.

Beneath the sprawling branches of a massive banyan tree, the venerable Rishi sat in quiet meditation upon a woven mat of kusha grass. The sage's silver matted hair was bound securely in a traditional Vedic *jata* topknot, dark rudraksha beads rested gently over his ascetic wrap, and three calm lines of sacred vibhuti marked his forehead.

Beside the granite step rested an earthen water vessel, and nearby on the dry soil, an errant scorpion crawled slowly past the rim before settling motionless in the dust.

"You carry a heavy cloud this morning, Madhava," the Sage said softly, opening his eyes with deep calm. "Has the harvest failed, or has the treasury ledger fallen into disorder?"

"Neither, Guruji," Madhava replied, bowing with clasped hands before taking a seat upon the lower stone slab. "The numbers are flawless, yet my work is unraveling. In our registry there is an official named Varun. For two years, I believed his friction with me was a matter of temper—a difference in habits, a misunderstanding of duties. I went out of my way to accommodate him. I named him in our joint accounts to the treasury, listened patiently to his grievances, and even defended him when others questioned his diligence. I thought reasonable benevolence would soften his rancor."

The Rishi watched the scorpion in the dust, listening intently.

"Yet yesterday," Madhava continued, his voice tight with bewilderment, "I discovered he spent weeks orchestrating a private campaign to scuttle a critical irrigation grant I designed. When confronted privately, he did not offer honest objections or a better plan. He simply smiled coldly and looked for the next lever to sabotage. Why does someone expend such boundless vitality merely to harm another? How can a person be so consumed by hostility when no offense was ever given?"

The Rishi gestured gently toward the ground between them.

"Look at the creatures of the wilderness, Madhava," the Sage began, his voice resonant and steady. "When a serpent slithers through the high grass, where does it carry its venom?"

"In its fangs, Guruji."

"And the scorpion resting beside this earthen jar—where does its poison reside?"

"In the sting of its tail," Madhava replied.

The Rishi nodded slowly. "Precisely. The serpent’s venom is localized in its teeth; it strikes only when stepped upon, startled, or hunting for sustenance. The scorpion carries venom only in the tip of its tail; outside of its defensive sting, its shell is harmless. But the ancient poet-yogi Vemana observed a darker reality about the unrighteous, envious human mind:

> **విషము చూడు పాము విషమందుననుండు**  
> **తేలుకును విషమ్ము తోకనుండు**  
> **దుర్జనునకు విషము తోడై శరీరమౌ**  
> **విశ్వదాభిరామ వినుర వేమ!**  

*‘The serpent carries poison only in its fangs; the scorpion carries venom only in its tail. But the malicious person is poison through and through—every thought, gesture, and fiber of their being is permeated with malice.’*"

Madhava listened in silence as the weight of the verse settled into the quiet morning air.

"When you deal with ordinary fellow officials who are weary, ambitious, or unsure of themselves," the Rishi explained, "their sharp words are like the scorpion’s tail: a single defensive strike under pressure. But the truly envious actor (*durjana*) does not strike out of passing fear or honest self-interest. To him, venom is not a weapon held in reserve; it is the air he breathes. Their malice is full-bodied."

## The Scorpion and the Serpent: Telling Them Apart

"The gravest mistake an honest servant of the crown can make," the Rishi went on, "is to lend his own good faith to a man who has none. When a decent official is attacked without cause, he tells himself:

1. *'If I explain myself more clearly, he will understand.'*
2. *'If I yield this point, or share more of the credit, he will feel safe.'*
3. *'If I keep showing goodwill, he will return it.'*

Vemana's verse warns that this is how good men are bitten twice. You must learn to tell an ordinary quarrel from rooted malice."

| | The ordinary difficult courtier | The *durjana* |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **What sets him off** | A fight over grain, a clash of duties, a hard season. | Your skill, your freedom, your honesty. Your very presence. |
| **Where he strikes** | At one matter in dispute, like the scorpion's tail. | At your name, your friends, and every work you begin. |
| **When you give ground** | He settles, and you find a fair middle. | He reads it as weakness and strikes harder. |
| **What he wants** | To finish his own task and keep his honest standing. | To pull you down, even if the whole registry suffers for it. |

## The Rishi's Counsel: Four Ways to Live Beside Venom

"Then what must I do about Varun?" Madhava asked.

### 1. Give Up Hope of Curing Him
Milk does not cure venom. You will not win over a man who feels threatened by your skill by giving him more of what he resents; every kindness becomes a stone in his sling. Treat his nature as you treat the monsoon or a rocky pass: something to plan around, not to plead with. Starting today, stop bargaining with him for his goodwill.

### 2. Put Everything in Writing and Narrow the Ground
His poison runs through every exchange, so spoken promises and corridor talk are where you will be stung.
- **Write it down:** after each dealing with him, send a short, plain note to the registry stating what was agreed, and keep a copy under your seal.
- **Close the side doors:** do not talk freely with him at festivals or in the courtyard, where your words can be carried off and twisted.
- **Keep the tallies in the open:** let every review of the irrigation grant rest on the measured figures, laid before the revenue council.

### 3. Do Not Feed the Fire
What he wants most is to see you shaken. If he provokes you into an angry outburst, he has pulled you onto ground where the loudest voice wins, not the truest account. Vidura taught that the wise stay calm on the surface while making their footing secure below. Answer his barbs with the same even courtesy you give the gatekeeper. A fire that is given no wood burns itself out.

### 4. Let Many Eyes See Your Work
A man who trusts only the quality of his own work is the easiest target for whispers. This month, carry your irrigation plans in person to the elders of the revenue council and to the heads of the neighbouring registries, and walk the canal site with the village headmen. When many honest witnesses can speak for your work, one man's venom cannot poison the whole well.

## The Rishi's Parting Words

The morning breeze moved through the banyan as the Rishi rose and laid a hand on Madhava's shoulder.

"Do not poison your own spirit by asking why venom is bitter, Madhava," he said gently. "The scorpion does what is in its nature, and the envious courtier does what is in his. Your duty is not to change his soul, but to keep your dharma and finish your work."

"Step around the scorpion. Guard your boundaries. Let your quiet skill be the granite on which his malice wears itself out."

Madhava bowed low. He walked back down the path toward the revenue registry, not with bitterness or false hope, but with clear eyes and a steady step.

> *"The serpent bites with its fangs and the scorpion with its tail, but the envious man is venom from head to heel; do not offer him your hand, only your quiet distance."*
